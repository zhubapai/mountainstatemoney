401(k) and 403(b): The Basics

Same idea, two names, depending on who signs your paycheck. Here's how employer retirement plans actually work, and the one feature you should never leave on the table.

7 min read Educational only

What these accounts actually are

A 401(k) or 403(b) is a retirement account offered through your employer, funded by automatic payroll deduction. The 401(k) is used by most private-sector employers; the 403(b) is the equivalent for employees of schools, universities, hospitals, and other nonprofit or government organizations. Functionally, for the employee, they work almost identically — the different name is really about which section of tax law applies to your employer.

Traditional or Roth — often your choice within the plan

Many employer plans let you choose between two tax treatments for your own contributions:

  • Traditional (pre-tax): Your contribution comes out of your paycheck before income tax is calculated, lowering your taxable income today. The money grows tax-deferred, and you pay ordinary income tax on withdrawals in retirement.
  • Roth: Your contribution comes out after tax — no upfront tax break. In exchange, the money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are also tax-free.

The Roth vs. Traditional IRA article covers the reasoning behind choosing one over the other in more depth — the same logic generally applies here.

The employer match: don't leave this on the table

Many employers match a portion of what you contribute — commonly something like "50% of the first 6% you contribute," though formulas vary widely by employer. This match is effectively an immediate, guaranteed return on your contribution before any investment growth even happens.

A concrete example: Say you earn $50,000 a year and your employer matches 50% of your contributions up to 6% of your salary. If you contribute 6% ($3,000), your employer adds another $1,500 — money you didn't have before, just for participating. Contributing less than 6% in this scenario means walking away from part of that match entirely. This is why "get at least the full match" is close to universal advice, regardless of what else you're doing with your money.

One catch worth knowing: employer contributions are sometimes subject to a vesting schedule, meaning you only fully own the matched funds after working there a certain number of years. Your own contributions are always 100% yours immediately — vesting schedules only apply to what the employer adds.

How much you can contribute

Contribution limits are set by the IRS and typically adjust for inflation each year. For 2026:

  • $24,500 — the employee contribution limit
  • $8,000 additional — catch-up contributions for those age 50 and up (total $32,500)
  • $11,250 additional — an enhanced catch-up for ages 60–63 specifically, if the plan allows it (total $35,750)
  • $72,000 — the combined cap including both employee and employer contributions

The tradeoff: your money is tied up until retirement age

These accounts are designed for retirement, and the tax code enforces that: withdrawing money before age 59½ generally triggers a 10% early withdrawal penalty on top of any regular income tax owed, with a handful of specific exceptions (certain hardships, disability, and a few other cases). This is part of why the emergency fund and shorter-term savings goals covered in Money Basics belong outside these accounts, not inside them.

This article is general financial education, not personalized advice. Contribution limits shown are for 2026 and are set by the IRS, subject to change in future years — confirm current limits and your specific plan's rules with your employer or a tax professional.
