Taxable Brokerage Accounts — When They Make Sense

No tax break going in — but also no rules about when you can touch your money. That tradeoff is exactly what makes this account type useful, in the right situation.

6 min read Educational only

What makes it different

A taxable brokerage account is just a regular investment account — no special tax treatment, no contribution limits, no income restrictions, and no penalty for withdrawing money whenever you want. You contribute already-taxed money, buy whatever investments you choose, and pay tax along the way as you earn dividends or sell for a gain. In exchange for giving up the tax advantages of a 401(k) or IRA, you get complete flexibility.

How the tax actually works

Two things get taxed in a brokerage account: dividends you receive, and gains when you sell an investment for more than you paid.

  • Long-term capital gains — profit on investments held longer than one year — get preferential tax rates: 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your income. For 2026, single filers pay 0% up to $49,450 of taxable income, 15% from there up to $545,500, and 20% above that (roughly double those thresholds for married couples filing jointly).
  • Short-term capital gains — profit on investments held one year or less — are taxed as ordinary income, at your regular tax bracket (10% to 37%), which is often meaningfully higher than the long-term rate.
Why the holding period matters: Say you're in the 22% ordinary income bracket and have a $5,000 gain on an investment. Sell it after holding one year or less, and that gain is taxed as ordinary income — roughly $1,100 in tax at 22%. Hold it just past the one-year mark instead, and it likely qualifies for the 15% long-term rate — roughly $750 in tax on the same gain. Same investment, same profit, $350 different purely based on how long you held it.

Where it fits in the overall picture

For most people, a taxable brokerage account makes the most sense after capturing the easy wins in tax-advantaged accounts first:

  1. Contribute enough to your 401(k)/403(b) to get the full employer match (see that article — this is free money).
  2. Consider maxing out an IRA (Traditional or Roth) for the additional tax advantage.
  3. Go back and contribute more to your 401(k)/403(b) up to its full limit, if you're able.
  4. Then a taxable brokerage account becomes the natural next place for additional investing — especially for goals before retirement age, since it has none of the age-59½ restrictions those other accounts carry.

It's also simply the only option once you've maxed out the contribution limits on the tax-advantaged accounts, or if you want to invest toward a goal that's still years away but not specifically retirement — a house down payment on a longer timeline, for instance.

One flexibility unique to taxable accounts

Because gains and losses in a taxable account are visible to the IRS every year, investors can use a strategy called tax-loss harvesting — selling an investment at a loss to offset taxable gains elsewhere, reducing the tax bill for the year. This isn't available inside a 401(k) or IRA, since those accounts aren't taxed year to year in the first place.

This article is general financial education, not personalized tax or investment advice. Capital gains tax brackets shown are for 2026 and are subject to change — confirm current rates and how they apply to your situation with a tax professional.
