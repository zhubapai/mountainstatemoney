Common Mistakes New Investors Make

Most investing mistakes aren't about picking the wrong stock. They're behavioral — and they repeat across nearly every new investor in some form.

7 min read Educational only

Trying to time the market

Waiting for the "right moment" to invest, or trying to guess when a downturn will end before getting back in, feels prudent but rarely works out — even professionals struggle to do this consistently. As covered in the dollar-cost averaging article, missing just the 10 best days in the market over a 20-year stretch has historically cut returns by more than half, and many of those best days occur during the same downturns that scare people out of the market in the first place.

Going all-in on one stock

A single exciting company — or a big pile of employer stock through a workplace plan — can feel like the obvious place to concentrate money, especially after it's already gone up. This is the exact risk the diversification article covers: one company's bad news shouldn't be able to sink your entire portfolio.

Ignoring fees

A 0.5–1% difference in expense ratio feels small enough to ignore, but as shown in the mutual funds vs. ETFs article, that gap can cost tens of thousands of dollars over decades, purely through compounding working against you instead of for you. Fees are one of the few things about future returns you can actually know in advance — worth checking before anything else.

Panic-selling during a downturn

A market drop only becomes a permanent loss once you sell. Selling out of fear during a decline locks in the loss and typically means missing the recovery that follows — the risk-tolerance article covers why building a portfolio you can actually sit through matters more than building the theoretically "optimal" one on paper.

Not having an emergency fund first

Investing before covering short-term needs sets up a bad outcome: an unexpected expense forces selling investments at whatever price the market happens to offer that day — possibly a loss — instead of coming out of cash that was never supposed to be invested in the first place. The Money Basics section covers why this order of operations matters.

Chasing whatever just performed well

It's tempting to pile into whatever stock, fund, or sector had a great year, assuming the trend continues. Strong recent performance doesn't predict future performance, and by the time a trend is obvious enough to notice, much of the gain has often already happened. A consistent plan, followed steadily, tends to beat chasing last year's winners.

The thread connecting all of these: Nearly every item on this list is a version of the same mistake — letting a short-term emotional reaction (excitement, fear, FOMO) override a plan that was reasonable when you made it with a clear head. The specific mistake changes, but the fix is usually the same: decide your approach in advance, automate what you can, and change course based on your actual goals and time horizon — not based on what the market did last week.
