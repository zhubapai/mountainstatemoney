Roth vs. Traditional IRA

An IRA isn't tied to any employer — you open one yourself at a brokerage. The real decision is when you'd rather pay the tax: now, or later.

7 min read Educational only

What an IRA is, and isn't

An IRA (Individual Retirement Account) is a retirement account you open on your own — through a brokerage, not through an employer — and fund with your own contributions. It's not tied to a job, so you keep the same IRA no matter how many times you change employers. It's a separate, additional option alongside (not instead of) any 401(k) or 403(b) you have access to at work.

The core tradeoff: pay tax now, or pay tax later

  • Traditional IRA: Contributions may be tax-deductible in the year you make them (this can be limited if you or a spouse are also covered by a workplace retirement plan and your income is above certain thresholds). The money grows tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
  • Roth IRA: Contributions are made with money you've already paid tax on — no deduction today. The money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement — including all the growth — are completely tax-free.

The single biggest factor in choosing between them: do you expect to be in a higher or lower tax bracket in retirement than you are right now? If you expect your tax rate to be lower in retirement (a common assumption for high earners in their peak working years), Traditional can make more sense — take the deduction now while your rate is high, pay tax later at a lower rate. If you expect to be in the same or a higher bracket later (common for people early in their careers, or anyone who thinks tax rates in general may rise), Roth can make more sense — pay tax now while your rate is known and comparatively low.

A simplified side-by-side: Say you have $6,000 to contribute and you're in a 22% tax bracket both now and in retirement. With a Traditional IRA, you contribute the full $6,000 pre-tax; it grows and is taxed at 22% on withdrawal. With a Roth IRA, you'd first pay 22% tax on that $6,000 of income, leaving $4,680 to actually contribute, but it's never taxed again. At the same tax rate both now and later, the two options produce the same after-tax result — the difference only shows up when your tax rate actually changes between now and retirement.

How much you can contribute

For 2026, the combined contribution limit across all your IRAs (Traditional and Roth added together) is $7,500, or $8,600 if you're age 50 or older. Roth IRAs also have income limits — for 2026, the ability to contribute directly phases out between $153,000–$168,000 of income for single filers, and $242,000–$252,000 for married couples filing jointly. Above the upper threshold, direct Roth contributions aren't allowed (though other strategies exist for higher earners — worth a conversation with a tax professional if this applies to you).

A practical Roth feature worth knowing

Roth IRA contributions (not the earnings on them) can generally be withdrawn at any time, tax- and penalty-free, since you already paid tax on that money. This doesn't make a Roth IRA a substitute for an emergency fund — the earnings portion has its own rules and penalties for early withdrawal — but it's a meaningfully more flexible account than a Traditional IRA if you're weighing the two.
