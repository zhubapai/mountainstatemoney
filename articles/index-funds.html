What's an Index Fund?

The least exciting-sounding idea in investing — just buy the whole market and don't try to beat it — is also one of the best-supported by the data.

6 min read Educational only

The idea in one sentence

An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF built to hold the same stocks, in the same proportions, as a specific market index — like the S&P 500 (roughly the 500 largest U.S. public companies). Instead of a manager trying to pick winners, the fund simply mirrors the index. If a company is added to or removed from the index, the fund adjusts to match. No one is trying to beat the market — the fund's goal is to be the market, as closely and cheaply as possible.

This is what "passive" investing means, as opposed to "active" investing, where a fund manager researches and picks specific holdings to try to outperform a benchmark.

Why costs are so much lower

Running an index fund is largely mechanical — there's no team of analysts trying to forecast which stocks will outperform, just software that keeps the fund's holdings matched to the index. That's the direct reason index funds can charge a fraction of what actively managed funds charge (see the mutual funds vs. ETFs article for the actual expense ratio numbers).

Why "just match the market" tends to win

This sounds like giving up on the chance to beat the market — and it is. The case for it comes down to what the data actually shows: according to S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA Scorecard, over the 15 years ending December 31, 2024, 89.5% of actively managed U.S. large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500. Not a close split — the large majority of professional fund managers, over long periods, did not beat a simple index fund that just holds everything.

Why active management struggles to win consistently: Every actively managed fund has to overcome its own costs before it can beat the index — a fund charging 0.64% a year has to generate returns that clear that hurdle just to tie an index fund charging 0.05%. Add in the difficulty of consistently picking winning stocks ahead of time, across an entire market full of professional investors doing the same thing, and the long-run numbers stop being surprising.

What this means practically

  • A single low-cost index fund tracking a broad market (like a total U.S. stock market index or an S&P 500 index fund) gives instant diversification across hundreds of companies.
  • Lower costs mean more of the market's return stays in your account, compounding over time, rather than going toward management fees.
  • This doesn't mean every actively managed fund is bad, or that no one ever beats the market — some do, especially over shorter periods. It means the odds, especially over long horizons, favor the simple, low-cost approach.
