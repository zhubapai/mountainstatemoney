Mutual Funds vs. ETFs

Both let you buy a basket of investments in one purchase instead of picking stocks one at a time. The differences that actually matter are more practical than philosophical.

6 min read Educational only

What they have in common

A mutual fund and an ETF (exchange-traded fund) are both pooled investments: your money is combined with other investors' money and used to buy a basket of stocks, bonds, or other assets — often dozens or hundreds of them in a single fund. Buy one share of the fund, and you instantly own a small slice of everything it holds. That's diversification without having to research and buy each holding individually.

Where they actually differ

  • How they trade: ETFs trade throughout the day on an exchange, just like a stock — the price moves in real time and you can buy or sell any time markets are open. Mutual funds are priced once per day, after markets close, based on the net asset value (NAV) of everything they hold. You can place an order any time, but it fills at that day's single closing price.
  • Minimum investment: Many mutual funds require a minimum initial investment (often $500–$3,000, though this varies widely). ETFs typically have no minimum beyond the price of one share, which makes them more accessible for smaller amounts.
  • Tax efficiency: ETFs are generally more tax-efficient in a taxable brokerage account due to how they're structured and redeemed, which tends to generate fewer taxable capital gains distributions than mutual funds. This mostly matters outside of tax-advantaged retirement accounts.
  • Where you'll encounter them: Employer retirement plans (401(k)s, 403(b)s) commonly offer mutual funds as the available options. Regular brokerage accounts commonly favor ETFs, though both are widely available in most places today.

Active vs. passive isn't the same question

It's easy to mix this up: "mutual fund vs. ETF" is a question about structure and trading mechanics. "Active vs. passive" is a separate question about strategy — whether a fund manager is actively picking investments to try to beat the market, or the fund is simply built to track a market index automatically. Both mutual funds and ETFs come in active and passive versions. The next article, on index funds, is entirely about that second question.

The cost difference is the part to actually pay attention to

Every fund — mutual fund or ETF — charges an ongoing fee called the expense ratio, taken automatically out of the fund's returns. It's expressed as a percentage per year. As of 2025, the average index equity mutual fund charges about 0.05% a year, while the average actively managed equity mutual fund charges about 0.64% — over 10 times more.

Why a fraction of a percent matters: On a $10,000 investment held for 30 years at a 7% gross annual return, a 0.05% expense ratio leaves you with roughly $75,100. The same investment at a 0.64% expense ratio leaves you with roughly $63,600 — a difference of over $11,000, purely from fees, on the exact same underlying return. Fees compound against you the same way returns compound for you.

The expense ratio matters more than whether something is technically a "mutual fund" or an "ETF" — a low-cost index mutual fund can easily beat a high-cost actively managed ETF, and vice versa. Always check the expense ratio, regardless of which wrapper the fund comes in.

This article is general financial education, not personalized investment advice. Expense ratio figures are recent industry averages and vary by specific fund — check any fund's actual prospectus for its current fee.
