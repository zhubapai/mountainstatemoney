What Is a Bond, Actually?

Bonds get called "boring" and "safe" in the same breath, which undersells them. They're really just loans — and once you see them that way, the rest clicks into place.

7 min read Educational only

A bond is a loan, and you're the bank

When you buy a bond, you're lending money to whoever issued it — a company, a city, or the federal government. In exchange, the issuer promises two things: to pay you regular interest (called the coupon) on a fixed schedule, and to return your original investment (the face value, or par value) when the bond matures on a set date.

A simple example: a 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value and a 4% coupon pays you $40 a year (usually split into two $20 payments) for 10 years, then returns your original $1,000 at the end. If you hold it to maturity and the issuer doesn't default, that's the entire transaction — no mystery.

Why bond prices move — and why that confuses people

Bonds can also be bought and sold before maturity, and this is where it gets less intuitive: bond prices move opposite to interest rates. When rates in the broader market rise, existing bonds with lower fixed coupons become less attractive, so their price has to drop for their effective yield to compete with new bonds. When rates fall, existing bonds with higher fixed coupons become more valuable, so their price rises.

A concrete example: Take that same $1,000 face value, 4% coupon, 10-year bond. If new bonds start being issued at 5% because market rates rose, nobody will pay full price ($1,000) for your 4% bond — so its market price drops to roughly $923, the price at which its fixed $40/year payments work out to a competitive 5% yield for a new buyer. If instead market rates fell to 3%, your 4% bond becomes more attractive than new bonds, and its price rises to roughly $1,085. The coupon payment never changes — the price adjusts around it.

This only matters if you sell before maturity. Hold a bond to maturity and none of this price movement affects what you ultimately receive, assuming the issuer pays as promised.

Not all bonds carry the same risk

  • U.S. Treasury bonds — issued by the federal government, generally considered the benchmark for "low risk" since default is considered extremely unlikely.
  • Municipal bonds ("munis") — issued by states or cities, often with tax advantages on the interest earned.
  • Corporate bonds — issued by companies, with risk that varies widely by issuer. A well-established company's bond carries less risk than a struggling one's.

Credit rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) grade bonds from investment-grade (lower risk) down to "junk" or high-yield (higher risk, higher promised return to compensate). A higher coupon on a bond is often the market's way of pricing in higher risk that the issuer won't pay it back — not simply a better deal.

Why bonds still aren't "risk-free"

Bonds are generally lower-risk than stocks — bondholders get paid before stockholders if a company runs into trouble — but "lower risk" isn't "no risk." Three risks worth knowing:

  • Credit/default risk — the issuer might not pay you back, particularly with lower-rated corporate bonds.
  • Interest rate risk — as shown above, a bond's market value can drop if you need to sell before maturity and rates have risen.
  • Inflation risk — a fixed coupon locked in today buys less in the future if inflation runs hot (see the inflation article in Money Basics).
