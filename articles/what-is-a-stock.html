What Is a Stock?

You've heard "buy stocks" your whole life without anyone necessarily explaining what you're actually buying. Here's the plain version.

6 min read Educational only

Ownership, not a bet

A stock (also called a "share" or "equity") is a small piece of ownership in a company. When a company sells stock, it's not borrowing money — it's selling a slice of itself to raise cash, usually to fund growth. If a company has issued 10 million shares and you own 100 of them, you own 0.001% of that company. Small, but real: you have a legal claim on a sliver of its future profits and, in theory, a vote on certain company decisions.

This is the core distinction worth holding onto: buying a stock isn't placing a bet on a number going up. It's buying a fractional ownership stake in an actual business, and the stock price is the market's constantly-updating guess at what that stake is worth.

How you actually make (or lose) money

There are two ways a stock can put money in your pocket:

  • Price appreciation — you buy a share for $50 and later sell it for $70. The $20 difference is your gain (and it's taxed as a capital gain when you sell).
  • Dividends — some companies pay out a portion of their profits directly to shareholders, usually quarterly, simply for holding the stock. Not all companies do this — many growth-focused companies reinvest profits instead of paying dividends.

Both directions work the same way in reverse: the price can fall below what you paid, and if the company fails outright, shares can become worthless. Unlike a bank account, there's no floor and no guarantee.

What "market cap" means: A company's market capitalization is simply share price × total shares outstanding. A company with 10 million shares trading at $50 each has a $500 million market cap — the market's current price tag on the entire business. This is why a $50 stock isn't automatically "cheaper" than a $500 stock; what matters is the price relative to the size and earnings of the company behind it, not the sticker price of one share.

What actually moves the price

Day to day, a stock's price is just the point where a buyer and a seller agree to trade. Zoom out, and prices tend to track a mix of:

  • Company performance — revenue, profit, and whether the business is growing or shrinking
  • Expectations — stock prices react to whether results beat or miss what investors already expected, not just whether they were "good"
  • Broader market and economic conditions — interest rates, inflation, overall investor sentiment can move most stocks together, regardless of any one company's news

This is why a company can report a genuine profit increase and still see its stock price drop — if investors expected even more, the "miss" relative to expectations is what moves the price.

Where stockholders stand if a company fails

It's worth knowing the pecking order: if a company goes bankrupt, bondholders (lenders) and other creditors get paid back first from whatever assets remain. Stockholders — the owners — are last in line, which is precisely why stocks carry more risk than bonds issued by the same company, and why they've historically offered higher potential returns to compensate for that risk.
