Emergency Funds: How Much, and Where to Keep It

The most boring part of personal finance is also the part that keeps a bad month from turning into bad debt. Here's how to size one and where it actually belongs.

5 min read Educational only

What actually counts as an emergency

An emergency fund is for things that are both necessary and unplanned: a job loss, a medical bill, a car repair that keeps you employed, a home repair that can't wait. It's not for a planned expense you just didn't budget for — a holiday, a vacation, a sale on something you wanted. If it's foreseeable, it belongs in regular budgeting, not the emergency fund.

How much is actually enough

The common guideline is 3 to 6 months of essential expenses — rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments. Not your full current spending, just what it would cost to keep the lights on if income stopped. A few things that push you toward the higher end of that range:

  • Self-employed or commission-based income, where earnings are less predictable
  • Being the sole income in a household
  • Working in a field or region with less job stability
  • Owning a home or car that's more likely to need an expensive repair

A dual-income household with stable jobs and no dependents can reasonably lean toward 3 months. A single-income household with a mortgage and an older car leans toward 6 — or more.

Where to actually keep it

This is the part people get wrong most often: an emergency fund does not belong in the stock market. The entire point of this money is that it has to be there, at close to its full value, exactly when you need it — which might be during a market downturn, since job losses and recessions often happen together. The right home for it is boring on purpose:

  • A high-yield savings account — FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, and pays meaningfully more interest than a typical checking or brick-and-bank savings account, while still being accessible within a day or two.
  • A money market account — similar safety and accessibility, sometimes with check-writing or debit access.
  • Short-term CDs — can work for a portion of a larger emergency fund, but the money is less accessible until the term ends, so this isn't ideal for the whole fund.

What all three have in common: the balance doesn't drop when the stock market does. That's not a coincidence — it's the actual requirement for this money.

A reasonable way to build one from zero: Start with a smaller target — $500 to $1,000 — before worrying about the full 3–6 month goal. That starter amount covers most small emergencies (a flat tire, a minor repair) and stops them from turning into credit card debt while you keep building toward the full fund. Automating a fixed transfer to a separate high-yield savings account each payday, even a small one, tends to work better than "saving whatever's left over," which is often nothing.

Where this fits with everything else

This closes out Money Basics: saving and investing are different tools for different jobs, compounding rewards money that's been invested the longest, inflation quietly erodes cash that just sits still, and an emergency fund is the deliberate exception — cash that's supposed to just sit still, because that's exactly its job.
