Compound Interest, Explained With Real Numbers

Everyone's heard that compound interest is powerful. Few people have actually seen the numbers laid out side by side. Once you do, "start now" stops being a cliché and starts being obvious math.

7 min read Educational only

What compounding actually means

Simple interest pays you on your original amount only. Compound interest pays you on your original amount plus whatever interest you've already earned — so your interest starts earning its own interest. It's a small mechanical difference that turns into a large practical one, purely because of time.

The same monthly amount, three different starting points

Say someone invests $200 a month at an average 7% annual return (a commonly used long-run estimate for a diversified stock portfolio — real years vary a lot, some much higher, some negative). Here's roughly where that ends up:

  • 10 years: about $24,000 contributed → grows to roughly $34,600
  • 20 years: about $48,000 contributed → grows to roughly $104,200
  • 30 years: about $72,000 contributed → grows to roughly $244,000

Notice what's happening: contributions only triple from the 10-year mark to the 30-year mark, but the total grows more than seven-fold. That gap between "money you put in" and "money you end up with" is compounding doing its work — and it does almost none of that work in the early years, and most of it in the later ones.

Why starting early matters more than the amount

Two people, same monthly contribution, different start dates: Person A invests $200/month starting at age 25 and stops entirely at 35 (10 years of contributions, $24,000 total), then leaves that money untouched until retiring at 65. Person B waits until 35 to start and contributes $200/month all the way to 65 (30 years, $72,000 total — three times as much). Assuming 7% average annual returns, Person A ends up with roughly $281,000 at 65. Person B ends up with roughly $244,000. Person A comes out ahead — with a third of the money put in — purely because those dollars had an extra decade to compound.

This isn't an argument that starting late is pointless — Person B still built a quarter-million dollars from disciplined saving, which is a real result. It's an argument that time in the market is doing more of the heavy lifting than most people assume, which is exactly why "I'll start once I have more to invest" is usually the more expensive plan.

The flip side: debt compounds too

Compounding isn't only a wealth-building tool — it works the same way in reverse on debt you carry. Credit card balances typically compound daily or monthly at rates far higher than any savings account pays, which is why a balance that isn't paid off can grow surprisingly fast even without new charges. The same math that builds a retirement account can quietly work against you on a revolving balance.

The figures above use a flat 7% annual return for illustration. Real investment returns vary year to year and are never guaranteed — this is general financial education, not personalized investment advice or a projection of what any specific investment will do.
